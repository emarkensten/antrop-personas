# Cooper persona methodology — quick notes

When the user invokes Cooper, this is the version we apply.

## What makes a Cooper persona

A Cooper persona is a **design target**: a single composite psychological profile that a strategy / copy / service team can hold in their head when making decisions. Not a marketing segment, not a demographic average.

Key Cooper conventions we follow:

- Composites are built from clusters of 2–4 real participants. The composite is named, given a single age, given a single life-context sketch — but the source members are *visible* in the doc (composite-from line in mono).
- Each persona has a single **end goal** (life-level) plus **experience goals** (in-the-moment) and **practical goals** (task-level). End goals do most of the design work.
- **Negative personas** are explicit. They name who the work is *not* for — protects the primaries from being diluted.
- **Edge cases stay visible**. Cooper's rule: edge cases are usually the most informative material for archetype design. Don't force every point onto the scale.

## How Cooper variables differ from jobs-to-be-done variables

| | Cooper | JTBD |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Person's life context, goals, frustrations | The job the person is hiring the product to do |
| Cuts data by | Goals + behaviour patterns | Functional, emotional, social progress sought |
| Output | 2–4 design-target personas + 1 negative + outlier notes | Job statements + force diagrams |
| Best for | Behaviour-change campaigns, service design, brand strategy | Product feature prioritisation, market sizing |
| Risk | Personas drift from data over time | Jobs become so abstract they lose actionability |

For Naturvårdsverket-style behaviour-change projects: Cooper. For product feature work where the user already knows their value proposition: JTBD is often sharper.

## Cluster cohesion test

A valid Cooper cluster shares:
- A coherent **end-goal** (V1) — the cluster has one motivation, not several
- Convergence on the **behavioural spine** (V2–V5) — they actually do similar things
- Compatible **attitudes** (V6–V7) — they react to interventions in compatible ways

If a cluster meets all three, it can become an archetype. If it only meets V1 (same goal, different behaviour), it's a *motivation segment* not an archetype — treat as two clusters that share a goal.

## When NOT to use Cooper

- Sample <8 interviews — clusters aren't supported by enough data
- Research questions are entirely about a single moment of decision (use moment-by-moment JTBD instead)
- Client needs population estimates ("how many of our customers are Anna?") — Cooper produces design targets, not population estimates. Either run a follow-on quantitative study or use a different framework.
